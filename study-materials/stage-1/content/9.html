Introduction

Welcome to Lesson 09 — today we're diving into one of the most important topics in robotics: Sensors. Without sensors, a robot is basically just a fancy paperweight — it can't see, hear, or feel anything around it. Sensors are how robots interact with the real world.

In this lesson, we'll cover:

  • What sensors are and why robots need them
  • IR sensors — obstacle detection & line following
  • PIR sensors — motion detection using heat
  • Ultrasonic sensors — measuring distance with sound
  • LDR — light sensing
  • Calibration & a handy comparison table

What is a Sensor?

A sensor is a device that detects changes in the physical environment — like light, heat, distance, or motion — and converts that into an electrical signal the robot's microcontroller (like an ESP32 or Arduino) can understand and act on.

Why Robots Need Sensors

  • To perceive their surroundings
  • To avoid obstacles and not crash
  • To follow lines or paths
  • To detect motion (people, objects)
  • To measure distance accurately
  • To respond to light conditions
  • To make smart decisions in real time

Simple Analogy

Think of sensors as a robot's senses — just like your eyes, ears, and skin help you understand the world, sensors give a robot its "perception" of the environment.

Key Point

No sensors = robot is "blind." Sensors make robots autonomous!

The basic sensing flow has three steps:

  1. Physical Environment
  2. Sensor Detects Change
  3. Microcontroller Acts

IR Sensor — Infrared Sensor

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OMZacCLRt9A

Active sensor — obstacle detection.

An IR (Infrared) sensor uses infrared light to detect objects. It has two main parts — an IR transmitter (LED that emits IR light) and an IR receiver (photodiode that detects reflected IR light).

How It Works — Reflection Principle

The IR transmitter sends out invisible infrared light. When this light hits an object, it reflects back. The receiver detects this reflection. No reflection = no object. Reflection detected = obstacle present!

Obstacle Detection Mode

Place sensor facing forward on robot. If something is in front → IR reflects back → receiver gets signal → robot stops or turns. Simple and effective! Works well up to ~20–30 cm.

Line Following Mode

Point sensor downwards at the ground. White surface = reflects IR = HIGH signal. Black line = absorbs IR = LOW signal. Robot follows the line by reading these changes continuously.

Active sensor

It emits its own IR light and doesn't depend on ambient light. Very popular in beginner robotics projects!

PIR Sensor

Passive sensor — motion detection.

A PIR (Passive Infrared) sensor detects motion by sensing changes in infrared radiation (heat) emitted by objects — especially warm bodies like humans and animals. It's called "passive" because it doesn't emit anything itself — it only receives.

Working Principle

  • All objects above absolute zero emit thermal radiation
  • Humans emit IR around 9–10 μm wavelength
  • PIR has two sensing elements — detects change between them
  • When a warm body moves → differential change detected
  • Output goes HIGH for a set time
Note

PIR only detects movement, not stationary objects!

Applications

  • Security alarm systems
  • Automatic lights (on when you walk in)
  • Intruder detection robots
  • Smart home automation
  • People-counting systems

Common module: HC-SR501 — easy to use with Arduino/ESP32!

Signal Chain

  1. Human IR
  2. Fresnel Lens
  3. PIR Detects
  4. Output HIGH
  5. Alarm/Light

Ultrasonic Sensor — HC-SR04

How To Use Ultrasonic Sensors with Arduino! + Project Idea! (YouTube, 04:09)

Distance measurement — ~40 kHz sound waves.

The HC-SR04 is the most popular ultrasonic sensor in student robotics. It measures distance by sending out an ultrasonic pulse (~40 kHz — beyond human hearing) and timing how long the echo takes to return.

TRIG Pin

Send a 10 μs pulse from microcontroller to trigger the sensor to emit ultrasonic waves.

Ultrasonic Wave

Sensor emits 40 kHz sound burst that travels forward until it hits an object.

Echo Return

Sound bounces off object and returns. Sensor measures time of flight.

Distance Formula

Distance = (Time × Speed of Sound) ÷ 2

Speed of sound ≈ 343 m/s

Measurement Sequence

  1. Trigger Pulse
  2. Emit 40kHz Wave
  3. Echo Returns
  4. Measure Duration
Range and the ÷2

Range: 2 cm to 400 cm. Accuracy: ~3mm. Perfect for obstacle-avoidance robots! The "÷2" in the formula is because sound travels to the object AND back — so we only want half the total distance.

LDR — Light Dependent Resistor

What is an LDR? (Light Dependant Resistor) - Electronics Basics 26 (YouTube, 03:31)

Passive sensor — light sensing.

An LDR (Light Dependent Resistor), also called a photoresistor, is a component whose resistance changes based on the intensity of light falling on it. Simple, cheap, and super useful!

How Resistance Changes

  • Bright light → Resistance decreases (~500 Ω)
  • Darkness → Resistance increases (~1 MΩ)
  • More light = more current flows
  • Less light = very little current

Used in a voltage divider circuit with a fixed resistor to get a readable analog voltage (0–5V) for the microcontroller.

Applications

  • Automatic street lights (on at dusk)
  • Light-following robots (solar trackers)
  • Camera exposure control
  • Night/day detection circuits
  • Screen brightness auto-adjust
Slow response

LDRs are slow to respond — fine for ambient light, not for fast changes!

Sensor Reading & Calibration

Raw sensor readings are never perfect — they vary based on environment, component tolerances, and noise. Calibration means testing your sensor in your specific setup and finding the right threshold values to make reliable decisions.

  1. Read the Raw Values — Open Serial Monitor and watch sensor output in different conditions (bright/dark, near/far, with/without object). Note the range of values you see.
  2. Find Your Threshold — Pick a value in the middle of your observed range. Example: IR sensor reads 800 in open space and 200 near a wall → threshold ≈ 500. Above 500 = no obstacle. Below 500 = obstacle!
  3. Test & Adjust — Run your robot and observe. If it's too sensitive → adjust threshold. Test in real conditions — different lighting, surfaces, distances. Calibration is not a one-time thing!
Why Calibrate?

Two identical sensors can give different readings. Your room's lighting is different from the lab. Calibration makes your code work in your environment!

Quick Recap & Key Takeaways

Let's wrap up Lesson 09! Here are the most important things to remember before you move on to Lesson 10:

1. Sensors = Robot's Senses

Without sensors, robots are blind. Sensors convert physical world data into electrical signals the microcontroller can read.

2. IR — Reflect & Detect

Transmitter + Receiver. Reflects IR off objects. Used for obstacle detection AND line following. Active sensor.

3. PIR — Detect Heat Motion

Passive sensor. Detects changes in thermal radiation. Only works with moving warm objects. Great for security!

4. Ultrasonic — Echo Distance

HC-SR04 sends 40 kHz pulse, times the echo. Distance = (time × 343) ÷ 2. Range: 2–400 cm.

5. LDR — Light = Low Resistance

Bright = low resistance. Dark = high resistance. Use voltage divider. Great for light-following robots.

6. Always Calibrate!

Read raw values → find threshold → test in real conditions. Every sensor and environment is different.
