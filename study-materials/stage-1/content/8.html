Digital Signal — The Basics

Welcome to Lesson 08! This is where things get really interesting — we're diving into the three types of signals that every robotics beginner must understand. Digital signals (simple ON/OFF), analog signals (smooth and continuous), and PWM (the clever trick that lets digital pins fake analog output). By the end of these notes, you'll be reading sensors, controlling LEDs, and understanding exactly what's happening at the pin level.

A digital signal is the simplest type of electrical signal. It has only two possible states — no in-between, no gray area:

  • HIGH = 1 = ON = ~3.3V or 5V (depends on your board)
  • LOW = 0 = OFF = 0V (GND)

Think of it like a light switch — either fully ON or fully OFF. There's no "half-on" in the digital world. Your microcontroller's GPIO pins send and read these signals constantly.

Digital I/O

A pin configured as output sends HIGH/LOW. A pin as input reads whether a signal is HIGH or LOW. Super useful for buttons, LEDs, relays!

Analog Signal — Continuous World

An analog signal is continuous — it can take any value within a range. Unlike digital's two states, analog smoothly varies over time, just like the real world does!

Real-world examples

  • Temperature sensor output (smoothly rises and falls)
  • LDR / Light sensor (changes with light intensity)
  • Potentiometer knob position (0 to full rotation)
  • Microphone audio signal (constantly changing)

Most things in the physical world are analog. Our microcontrollers are digital — so we need a way to bridge the two worlds. That's where ADC comes in (more on that soon!).

PWM — Pulse Width Modulation

How PWM works | Controlling a DC motor with a homemade circuit (YouTube, 10:01)

PWM stands for Pulse Width Modulation. It's a clever technique where a digital pin rapidly switches between HIGH and LOW to simulate an analog output. The key idea: control how long the signal stays HIGH vs LOW in each cycle.

Key terms to know

  • Ton — Time the signal is HIGH (ON time)
  • Toff — Time the signal is LOW (OFF time)
  • Period (T) = Ton + Toff (one full cycle)
  • Frequency = 1 / Period

The faster the switching, the smoother the effect. On ESP32, PWM frequency is typically 1 kHz – 50 kHz.

Duty Cycle — The Heart of PWM

Duty Cycle tells us what percentage of one period the signal is HIGH. It's the number that controls brightness, speed, power — everything!

Formula

Duty Cycle = Ton ÷ (Ton + Toff) × 100%

25% Duty Cycle

Signal is HIGH for ¼ of the period. LED appears dim. Motor runs slow.

50% Duty Cycle

Signal is HIGH for ½ of the period. LED at half brightness. Medium speed.

75% Duty Cycle

Signal is HIGH for ¾ of the period. LED appears bright. Motor runs fast.

ADC — Analog to Digital Converter

Electronic Basics #27: ADC (Analog to Digital Converter) (YouTube, 06:48)

An ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter) is a circuit inside your microcontroller that converts a continuous analog voltage into a digital number the CPU can process. Without it, your MCU literally cannot read sensors like LDRs, temperature sensors, or potentiometers!

Why do we need it? The real world is analog (smooth voltages). Your ESP32 is digital (only understands 1s and 0s). ADC bridges the gap!

Resolution Formula

Resolution

Resolution = Vref ÷ 2n

where n = number of bits

ADC Resolution — 10-bit vs 12-bit

Higher bit resolution = more precise readings. Let's break it down with the formula Resolution = Vref / 2n (assuming Vref = 3.3V):

ADC TypeBits (n)Levels (2n)Resolution (at 3.3V)
10-bit ADC101024 levels3.3V ÷ 1024 ≈ 3.22 mV
12-bit ADC124096 levels3.3V ÷ 4096 ≈ 0.81 mV

10-bit Example (Arduino)

Input: 1.65V → Output: 512. Half of 1024 = half voltage. Simple!

12-bit Example (ESP32)

Input: 1.65V → Output: 2048. Half of 4096 = half voltage. More precise!

Remember

ESP32 has a 12-bit ADC by default (0–4095). Arduino Uno has 10-bit ADC (0–1023). More bits = finer detail in your sensor readings!

The 4 Essential Functions

Arduino Lesson 2 - digitalRead & digitalWrite (YouTube, 08:56)

These are the core functions you'll use in almost every robotics project. Know them well!

  1. digitalRead(pin)

    Reads a digital pin. Returns HIGH (1) or LOW (0). Used for buttons, switches, digital sensors.

  2. digitalWrite(pin, value)

    Sets a digital pin to HIGH or LOW. Used for LEDs, relays, buzzers. Example: digitalWrite(2, HIGH);

  3. analogRead(pin)

    Reads an analog pin via ADC. Returns a number: 0–1023 (Arduino) or 0–4095 (ESP32). Used for sensors.

  4. analogWrite(pin, value)

    Outputs PWM on a pin. Value: 0–255 (0 = off, 255 = full). Used for LED brightness, motor speed control.

Project: LDR Controls LED Brightness

Arduino Basics - #6 - analogRead() & analogWrite() (YouTube, 16:50)

Here's a real practical example that ties everything together — an LDR (Light Dependent Resistor) reading ambient light and using that value to control an LED's brightness via PWM. This is exactly how automatic night lights work!

How it works

  1. LDR output → analogRead(A0) → value 0–4095
  2. Map the value: map(val, 0, 4095, 0, 255)
  3. Send to LED: analogWrite(D5, brightness)
  4. More light = brighter LED. Less light = dimmer LED.
Components

ESP32, LDR + 10kΩ resistor (voltage divider), LED + 220Ω resistor, breadboard, jumper wires.

Bright Room

LDR reads HIGH → PWM ~200 → LED bright

Dark Room

LDR reads LOW → PWM ~30 → LED dim

Key Concept

Analog sensor → ADC → PWM output. Full circle!
