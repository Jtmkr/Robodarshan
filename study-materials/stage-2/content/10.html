From idea to board: the workflow and the tools

Until now your robot has been a sandwich of breakout boards and jumper wires. It works, until one wire slips out during a run. A printed circuit board (PCB) replaces the wires with copper traces, so the connections are permanent, small and repeatable. In this lesson you will design a carrier board: a PCB that holds your ESP32 DevKit, the motor driver, the ADC boards and all the connectors, so the robot becomes one neat unit.

Every PCB project, in any tool, follows the same path:

  1. Idea: list the parts, draw a block diagram, decide which pin does what.
  2. Schematic: draw the circuit as symbols and wires.
  3. ERC: the computer checks the schematic for electrical mistakes.
  4. Footprints: tell the tool the real copper pattern of every part.
  5. Layout and routing: place the parts on the board outline and draw the traces.
  6. DRC: the computer checks the board against the manufacturer's limits.
  7. Gerber and drill files: export what the factory needs, look at them, then order.

KiCad: the tool we use

The club teaches with KiCad, a free and open-source suite that runs on Windows, Linux and macOS. At the time of writing the stable series is KiCad 10 (check kicad.org for the current release). A KiCad project has a schematic editor, a PCB editor, a symbol and footprint library editor, a 3D viewer and a Gerber viewer, all started from one project window.

Menus change between versions

The menu names and shortcut keys in this lesson are the ones we know from recent KiCad versions. Your screen may differ slightly. If you cannot find a tool, use the search or the Help menu, and read the release notes of your version.

EasyEDA: the other tool in the syllabus

Schematic capture and footprints

The schematic says what is connected to what. It does not care where things sit on the board. Draw it neatly: a messy schematic hides mistakes.

Symbols, wires and labels

  • Symbol: the drawing of a part (resistor, header, IC). Add one with Place → Add Symbol (shortcut A). Your ESP32 DevKit can be a plain pin-header symbol; you only need its pins.
  • Wire: draw with W. A wire connects only where its end touches a pin end, or at a junction dot.
  • Net label: L places a name on a wire. All wires with the same label are connected, even if no wire joins them. Use labels such as SDA, SCL, ENC_L_A instead of long crossing wires.
  • Power symbols: P adds symbols like GND, +3V3, +5V. Every symbol with the same power name is connected together.
  • No-connect flag: Q marks a pin you deliberately leave unused, so the checker stays quiet about it.

Annotation and ERC

Annotation gives every part a unique name (R1, R2, C1…). Run it from Tools → Annotate Schematic. Then run the Electrical Rules Checker from Inspect → Electrical Rules Checker. The ERC finds mistakes that would be nasty on a real board. Common messages:

What is PWR_FLAG?

Power comes into the board through a connector, and a connector pin is not marked as a power source. The ERC therefore complains that your 12 V net is “not driven”. A PWR_FLAG symbol (add it with P) tells the checker “power really arrives here”. Put one on the battery + and GND nets and on the 5 V net fed by the buck module. It is only a note for the checker and adds no real part.

Do not just silence warnings. Read each one and understand it first; a warning you ignored is often the one that becomes a dead board.

Footprints: the most expensive mistake to get wrong

A footprint is the copper pattern (pads and holes) for a part. After the schematic is done, assign a footprint to every symbol (the footprint assignment tool, or the footprint field of each symbol).

  • Check against the real part. Put your actual DevKit, driver board and ADS1115 breakout next to a ruler or calipers. Measure the row spacing and the pin positions. Many modules look identical and are not.
  • The common header pitch is 2.54 mm (0.1 inch). An ESP32 DevKit has two rows of headers, and the distance between the rows differs between the 30-pin and 38-pin versions and between brands. Measure yours.
  • The bare ESP32-WROOM module (used later) has castellated pads at 1.27 mm pitch; use the footprint from the module datasheet or the official library.
  • Breakout boards (TB6612FNG, ADS1115, MPU6050, OLED) have the pins in a vendor-specific order. Do not trust a library footprint by name; check the order of the labels on your board.
  • To be safe, print the PCB footprints at 1:1 on paper and place the real parts on the printout. If the pins do not line up, find out now.
Library footprints can be wrong

A symbol or footprint from the internet is only as good as its author. A mirrored or shifted pin row is a board you cannot use. Always verify against the datasheet drawing or the real part.

The ESP32 carrier board for the line follower

Our first target is a carrier board that carries everything in the Stage 2 line follower. It does not replace the ESP32 DevKit: the DevKit plugs into sockets (female headers), so you can swap it if it breaks.

Assumptions in this lesson
  • ESP32 DevKit (classic ESP32-WROOM-32), TB6612FNG breakout, two ADS1115 breakouts, QTR-8A, MPU6050 and SSD1306 OLED modules, N20 motors with encoders, a 3S LiPo and a 12 V to 5 V buck module.
  • The pin map is the suggested one from the other Stage 2 lessons. It is a suggestion; change it if your build differs.
  • All module pin orders and sizes are examples. Replace them with measurements of your own parts.

Connection plan

What goes on the board

  • Sockets for the ESP32 DevKit, TB6612FNG breakout, two ADS1115 breakouts, MPU6050 and OLED. Add a solder jumper or a header for each ADS1115 ADDR pin: to GND for 0x48, to VDD for 0x49.
  • A connector for the QTR-8A: ground, power and the 8 outputs (and the optional LED-control pin if you use it). Power it from 3.3 V so its outputs never go above the ADC supply voltage. Check the module's own datasheet for its current and for the pin order.
  • Two 6-pin connectors for the N20 motors: two motor wires, encoder power, encoder ground, A and B. The order differs between vendors, so wire it to your motor's cable. Many encoders work from 3.3 V; confirm yours, as 5 V encoder outputs must never reach an ESP32 input. Add footprints for four optional 10 kΩ pull-up resistors on the encoder lines (the input-only pins 34 to 39 have no internal pull-ups).
  • Three buttons with no external resistors needed.
  • Battery input through an XT60 connector (match the plug on your LiPo), a power switch rated for the current, and a 12 V to 5 V buck module footprint. A 3S pack is 12.6 V when full, so check that your module accepts that input voltage and that its output is set to 5 V before the ESP32 is connected.
  • Test points for VBAT, 5 V, 3.3 V, GND, SDA and SCL.

Power and reverse-polarity protection

Connecting a LiPo backwards destroys things in a fraction of a second. Two usual fixes:

  • A series Schottky diode: simple, but it wastes about a third to half a volt and gets warm at motor currents.
  • A P-channel MOSFET on the positive line (battery to the drain, load on the source side, gate to ground through a resistor): it blocks a reversed battery and wastes very little power. Pick one with enough current rating, a low on-resistance at the gate voltage you will really have, and a gate-source limit above your battery voltage. Follow the datasheet and a reference circuit.

Also consider a fuse or resettable fuse on the battery input, and a bulk capacitor (for example 100 µF or more) near the motor driver VM pin. And never plug USB and the buck module into the DevKit at the same time without a protection diode or a way to disconnect one of them, because the two supplies can fight.

Safety

A LiPo can deliver very large current into a short and can catch fire. Check polarity on the connector with a multimeter before the first power-up, and use a current-limited bench supply or a fuse for the first test if you have one.

Layout: placing parts and planning copper

Open the PCB editor and load your design with Tools → Update PCB from Schematic (shortcut F8). All footprints appear in a heap, joined by thin lines called the ratsnest. These lines show what still needs a trace.

Board outline and mounting holes

  • Draw the board outline on the Edge.Cuts layer as one closed shape. Make it fit your chassis; rounded corners are nicer and safer.
  • Add mounting holes (M3 is common) at the corners, and keep parts away from them.
  • Remember connector sides: the QTR-8A and motors are on the front and sides, the battery and switch where you can reach them.

Placement strategy

  1. Place the large and fixed parts first: the DevKit sockets, connectors, the buck module, the XT60.
  2. Group parts by function. Keep the motor power parts (battery input, buck, TB6612, motor connectors) on one side and the logic and sensor parts (ESP32, ADS1115, MPU6050, OLED) on the other.
  3. Rotate parts so the ratsnest lines are short and do not cross much.
  4. Place the decoupling capacitors last-but-important: right next to the supply pin of each chip, with the short path to its ground.

Keep noise away from sensitive lines

Motors draw current in sharp bursts, and a trace that carries those bursts radiates and shares voltage with its neighbours. Keep motor power and motor wires away from the I²C lines, the encoder lines and the QTR-8A analog lines. Do not run a signal trace in parallel next to a motor trace for a long distance. If they must cross, cross at a right angle on different layers.

Decoupling, ground and copper pours

  • Decoupling capacitor: a small ceramic (typically 100 nF) from each IC supply pin to ground, placed as close as you can. It supplies the quick bursts of current so the supply line stays steady. Bulk capacitors (10 to 100 µF or more) go near the power entry and near the motor driver.
  • Ground plane: on a two-layer board, use one layer (usually the bottom) mostly as ground. Add a copper zone (Place → Zone, shortcut Ctrl+Shift+Z) on the GND net and fill it (B). Every ground pin then connects to it with a short via or a direct pad connection.
  • Star ground idea: do not let the motor return current flow through the sensor area. Arrange the board so that the motor ground and the logic ground meet near the battery connector, and the motor current takes the short way back from the driver to the battery.

Trace width for current

A trace is a wire made of a thin copper strip, and it heats up when current flows. The standard IPC-2221 relation for the cross-section area is

I = k × ΔT0.44 × A0.725

where I is current in A, ΔT the allowed temperature rise in °C, A the cross-section in square mils, and k is 0.048 for outer layers (0.024 for inner layers). With ordinary 1 oz copper (about 35 µm thick) on an outer layer and a 10 °C rise, this works out roughly to:

CurrentMinimum width (about)
0.5 A0.12 mm
1 A0.3 mm
2 A0.8 mm
3 A1.4 mm

These are minimums from a formula, and the formula is an approximation. A practical rule for this robot: 0.25 to 0.3 mm for signals, 0.5 mm or more for 3.3 V and 5 V rails, and 1.5 mm or wider (or a pour) for battery and motor power. Wider is almost always better and costs nothing. KiCad can also calculate widths for you in its built-in PCB calculator.

Vias, silkscreen and polarity marks

  • A via is a plated hole that moves a trace from one layer to another. Use as few as you can on signals, and several vias for a high-current connection.
  • The silkscreen is the printed text on the board. Label every connector (what it is and pin 1), mark the polarity of battery and capacitors, mark switch positions and write the board name and version. Keep text off the pads.
  • Label which ADS1115 is 0x48 and which is 0x49. Your future self will thank you.

Routing and DRC

Routing: drawing the traces

Routing turns each ratsnest line into a copper trace. In KiCad, start the interactive router from Route → Route Single Track (key X), click on a pad, and move the mouse. The router pushes around obstacles for you. You can also draw traces by hand, which gives more control.

  • Route the important nets first: power, then high-current motor nets, then sensitive signals, then the easy ones.
  • Use 45-degree bends and avoid 90-degree and acute angles. Sharp angles are harder to manufacture cleanly and can trap acid in the etching.
  • Keep traces short and direct. Do not loop around for no reason.
  • Keep the two I²C lines close to each other, and keep their total length short.
  • For wide power traces, use a zone or a thick trace rather than thin traces in parallel.
  • Avoid running signal traces under a socketed module's noisy parts, and leave gaps from the board edge.
Do not fight the router

If a trace will not fit, move a part. Layout and routing are one loop: change placement, rotate a part, or swap equivalent pins (for example, two motor direction pins on the DevKit) to untangle the board. If you swap pins, update the schematic and the code.

DRC: the design rules check

The Design Rules Checker (Inspect → Design Rules Checker) looks at your finished board and checks:

  • Clearance: copper that is too close to other copper (a possible short).
  • Minimum trace width, via size and drill size the manufacturer cannot make.
  • Unconnected nets: a trace you forgot.
  • Edge clearance: copper too near the board outline.
  • Silkscreen over pads and other manufacturing problems.

The numbers it checks against are your design rules, set in File → Board Setup → Design Rules. Do not guess them. Open your PCB manufacturer's website, find the capabilities page for the board type you will order (for example, a standard two-layer board), and enter its current minimum trace width, clearance, via and hole sizes. Capabilities change from time to time and between services, so we do not quote numbers here. Many manufacturers also publish a ready-made KiCad rules setup.

Fix every error. A warning might be fine, but you should know why. Run DRC again after each fix, and after refilling zones.

Gerber files, 3D view and ordering

What a Gerber is

The factory does not use your KiCad project. It uses Gerber files, an industry-standard format with one file per layer (copper, solder mask, silkscreen and the board outline), plus drill files that tell the machine where to drill holes.

Exporting from KiCad

  1. Refill all zones (B) and run DRC one last time.
  2. Open File → Plot. Choose Gerber as the format and tick the layers: F.Cu, B.Cu, F.Silkscreen, B.Silkscreen, F.Mask, B.Mask and Edge.Cuts. (Add the paste layers only if you order a stencil or assembly.)
  3. Choose an output folder and click Plot.
  4. Click Generate Drill Files. Use the Excellon format and generate the plated and non-plated holes. Many factories also want the drill map; follow their guide.
  5. Put all the files into one zip.
carrier-v1-gerbers.zip
  carrier-F_Cu.gbr          top copper
  carrier-B_Cu.gbr          bottom copper
  carrier-F_Mask.gbr        top solder mask
  carrier-B_Mask.gbr        bottom solder mask
  carrier-F_Silkscreen.gbr  top printing
  carrier-B_Silkscreen.gbr  bottom printing
  carrier-Edge_Cuts.gbr     board outline
  carrier-PTH.drl           plated holes
  carrier-NPTH.drl          non-plated holes (mounting holes)

The exact names depend on your KiCad version and the factory's preferences; the content is what matters.

Look before you order

  • Open the zip in the Gerber Viewer (a tool in the KiCad project window) or in the manufacturer's online viewer. Switch layers on and off. Check the outline, the holes and the mirrored text.
  • Open the 3D Viewer in the PCB editor (View → 3D Viewer, Alt+3). If your parts have 3D models, you can see whether the headers face the right way and whether tall parts collide. Rotate the board and look at it from every side.
  • Print the layout at 1:1 once more and put the real parts on it.

Ordering and assembly tips

  • Order from a manufacturer that publishes its capabilities, and read their file-upload guide. Most have an instant quote page where you upload the zip.
  • The basic choices are the number of layers (2 for us), thickness (1.6 mm is standard), copper weight (1 oz), solder mask colour and surface finish. Start with the defaults.
  • Boards often come in small minimum quantities, so order a few spares. Also order a few extra connectors and modules.
  • Some factories also assemble the small surface-mount parts. That is useful later, but our first carrier board uses mostly through-hole headers that you solder yourself.
  • When the boards arrive, do not plug in the DevKit first. Check for shorts with a multimeter in continuity mode between 5 V and GND, 3.3 V and GND, VBAT and GND, then power up without modules and check the rails.

The same carrier for the STM32F411 Black Pill

The STM32F411 Black Pill has many ADC pins and hardware encoder timers. A carrier board for it is almost the same board: the power, motor driver, connectors and mounting stay, only the signals move to different pins and the socket footprint changes. (A Black Pill has 40 pins in two rows; measure yours.)

What changes and why

  • Line sensor straight to ADC. The F411 has an ADC with 16 inputs, and on the Black Pill 10 are on pins PA0 to PA7, PB0 and PB1. All 8 QTR-8A outputs can go to PA0 to PA7, and the two ADS1115 chips (and their slow readings) can be dropped. On the carrier, keep the ADS1115 footprints as optional, or leave them out of the STM32 version.
  • Encoders on hardware timers. A timer in encoder mode counts the quadrature pulses by itself, with no interrupt code. We use TIM4 channels 1 and 2 on PB6/PB7 and TIM1 channels 1 and 2 on PA8/PA9.
  • PWM from TIM3 channels 3 and 4 on PB0/PB1. They do not clash with the other timers.
  • I²C moves to PB8 (SCL) and PB9 (SDA) of I2C1, so PB6/PB7 stay free for the encoder timer. The Arduino core's default I²C pins can differ, so say it in the code.
  • Logic level: the STM32 ADC pins measure up to 3.3 V. Power the QTR-8A from 3.3 V.
  • Do not use PA11/PA12 (USB), PA13/PA14 (SWD programming) or the crystal pins.
Verify before you commit

The pin and alternate-function assignments in the figure come from our reading of the STM32F411 datasheet's alternate-function table. Check each one yourself in the datasheet table and in your board's pinout, and also check that the pins you choose are not used by something on your Black Pill (an on-board LED, a button, the USB). Two of the button pins in our plan have other roles: PA10 is the USART1 receive pin and PB4 belongs to the JTAG debug port at reset, so test them early or pick other free pins.

Why have both sockets?

Some clubs design one carrier with both an ESP32 socket and a Black Pill socket, with a jumper choosing which is used. That is harder: the sockets share the same connectors and you must make sure only one board drives each net. For a first board, build one version and keep the schematic for the other.

Advanced: boards with a bare microcontroller

A carrier board uses a ready-made DevKit or Black Pill that already contains the power regulator, USB, crystal and reset circuit. A bare-MCU board puts the chip (or a bare module) on your PCB. It saves space and money, but you must design what the DevKit did. Here is the minimum for each target. Treat it as an overview and always follow the official hardware design guidelines and datasheet of the exact part.

Not for the first board

These boards need more experience: antenna keep-out areas, crystal layout, USB signals and a boot process that can refuse to start if one resistor is wrong. Beginners should build a carrier board first and make a bare-MCU board as a second project.

ESP32-WROOM module: the minimum

  • 3.3 V supply that can deliver enough current for Wi-Fi bursts (the module datasheet gives a recommended figure, around half an ampere for the whole board is a common guideline). Use a proper regulator, not a weak one.
  • Decoupling: a bulk capacitor (the guideline uses about 10 µF) plus a 100 nF capacitor, close to the module's 3V3 pin.
  • EN pin: the chip enable. Pull it up to 3.3 V with a resistor and add a capacitor to ground so it rises slowly at power-up. The reference design uses roughly 10 kΩ and 1 µF. Add a reset button from EN to ground if you wish.
  • GPIO0 (boot): low at reset means “download mode” and high means “run the program”. Add a button to ground, and leave it floating or pulled high in normal use.
  • Other strapping pins: GPIO12 must not be pulled high at boot on a module with 3.3 V flash, and GPIO2 and GPIO15 have boot roles. Do not attach strong external loads to them.
  • Programming: a USB-UART chip (the usual CP2102 or CH340 type) or just a 6-pin header (3V3, GND, TX0, RX0, EN, GPIO0) for an external adapter. The auto-reset circuit on DevKits uses two transistors to drive EN and GPIO0 from the adapter's DTR and RTS lines; copy the reference schematic.
  • Antenna: keep copper, ground pour, components and wires away from the antenna end of the module, as the datasheet shows.

STM32F411: the minimum

  • Supply decoupling: a 100 nF capacitor at every VDD pin, as close as you can, and a bigger one (the reference design suggests 4.7 µF) on the supply. VDDA (the analog supply) needs its own filtering.
  • VCAP: the chip has a core regulator that needs an external capacitor on the VCAP pin(s). The datasheet gives the value and the required low ESR; for the 48-pin F411 we remember a single 4.7 µF ceramic, but read your package's datasheet. Without it, the chip will not run.
  • HSE crystal: an external crystal (the Black Pill uses 25 MHz) between PH0 and PH1, with two load capacitors to ground. A common estimate is C = 2 × (CL − Cstray), where CL is the load capacitance in the crystal's datasheet and the stray part is a few pF. For USB the clock has to be accurate, so use a crystal.
  • BOOT0: pull it to ground with a resistor (for example 10 kΩ) for normal start. Pull it to 3.3 V at reset to enter the built-in bootloader. Keep PB2 (BOOT1) low.
  • Reset: the NRST pin has an internal pull-up; add a button and a 100 nF capacitor to ground.
  • SWD header: 3.3 V, GND, SWDIO (PA13), SWCLK (PA14) and NRST, for an ST-Link programmer. It is the most reliable way to program and debug.
  • USB: PA11 (D−) and PA12 (D+) to the connector, as a short matched pair, with the shield connected as in the reference design, and a regulator for 3.3 V.
  • Tie VBAT to VDD if you do not use a battery backup.

The values above are overviews from the manufacturers' guidelines as we understand them. Verify them against the current datasheet and reference manual before sending a board to be made.

Pre-order checklist and common beginner mistakes

Pre-order checklist

  • ERC has no unexplained errors; every power net has a PWR_FLAG where needed.
  • Every footprint has been compared with the real part (printed 1:1 and tested).
  • DRC passes with the manufacturer's current rules; zones are refilled.
  • Battery polarity, XT60 orientation and the power switch are correct, and reverse protection is in place.
  • Decoupling capacitors are next to each chip, and the motor power is separate from I²C and analog lines.
  • Trace widths suit the currents; mounting holes fit your chassis.
  • Connectors are labelled on the silkscreen, with pin 1 and polarity; version and date are on the board.
  • Test points exist for the main rails.
  • Gerbers and drill files were inspected in a viewer; the 3D view looks right.
  • The pin map in the schematic matches the pin map in your code.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Wrong footprint or mirrored header: the board does not fit the module.
  • Forgetting a connection because the ratsnest was not fully routed; always check the DRC for unconnected items.
  • Traces too thin for the motor current, which heat up or burn.
  • Not placing a capacitor at the chip, so the board resets randomly.
  • Using a strapping pin (GPIO0, 2, 12, 15) for something that pulls it the wrong way at boot.
  • Using the flash pins GPIO6 to GPIO11.
  • Powering a sensor from 5 V so that its output goes above 3.3 V.
  • Making the board before the pin map is tested on a breadboard. Test every module and the code on the breadboard first, then move to the PCB.
  • Skipping the test with a multimeter before the first power-up.
Plan for version 2

Almost nobody gets a board perfect the first time. Add a few spare pads and test points, expect to fix at least one small mistake, and keep the KiCad project in Git so you can see what you changed between versions.

Key takeaways

  • The flow is: idea, schematic, ERC, footprints, layout, routing, DRC, Gerbers, order. Both checks can send you back a step.
  • Use KiCad (menus differ slightly between versions); EasyEDA is a browser-based alternative with built-in parts and ordering, and the skills transfer between them.
  • Run ERC and understand every message. PWR_FLAG tells the checker where power comes in.
  • Check every footprint against the real part, and print it at 1:1.
  • Place decoupling capacitors next to the chip pins, keep motor power away from I²C, encoder and analog lines, and use a ground pour with a sensible return path.
  • Size traces for the current (IPC-2221 gives roughly 0.8 mm for 2 A on 1 oz outer copper at a 10 °C rise); use wider for power.
  • Set the DRC from your manufacturer's current capabilities, not from numbers someone quoted.
  • Export Gerber and drill files, inspect them in a viewer and the 3D view, and test the new board with a multimeter before you plug in expensive parts.
  • The STM32 carrier moves the signals to hardware timers and ADC pins; a bare-MCU board needs more experience, so start with a carrier board.
