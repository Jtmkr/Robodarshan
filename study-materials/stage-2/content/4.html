Differential drive: steering with two wheels

Almost every line follower and maze robot you will build uses differential drive: two driven wheels, one on each side, each with its own motor, plus a third point (a caster ball or a skid) to hold the body up. There is no steering wheel. You steer purely by making the two wheels turn at different speeds. This is exactly what your drive(left, right) function from Lesson 01 does.

Assumptions in this lesson

The club decision for the larger robot in this lesson is any Johnson-type 12 V geared DC motor rated 500 RPM, with a 65 mm wheel. Every number below that is not given by that sentence is an ASSUMPTION used only to make the worked examples concrete. Replace each one with a value from your own motor datasheet and your own robot:

  • Robot mass: 1.2 kg (assumption). Weigh your robot on a kitchen scale.
  • Wheel base (distance between the two wheel contact patches): 140 mm (assumption).
  • Usable (continuous) torque per motor at the wheel: 1.0 kg·cm (assumption). Stall torque: 3.0 kg·cm (assumption). Johnson motors vary a lot between makers.
  • Rubber tyre on a clean, smooth track: friction coefficient μ = 0.7 (assumption; you will measure yours later in this lesson).
  • About 60 % of the weight sits on the driven wheels (assumption).

The three cases

  • Equal speeds (vR = vL): the robot drives straight. Both forward means forward, both backward means reverse.
  • Unequal speeds: the robot drives along a curve, turning towards the slower wheel. If the right wheel is faster, the robot curves left.
  • Opposite speeds (vR = −vL): the robot spins on the spot around the middle of the axle.

The formulas

Let L be the wheel base and vL, vR the speeds of the two wheels at the ground (in m/s). Then:

  • Forward speed of the robot centre: v = (vR + vL) / 2
  • Turning rate: ω = (vR − vL) / L in radians per second (positive means turning left, counter-clockwise)
  • Turning radius of the centre: R = v / ω = (L / 2) × (vR + vL) / (vR − vL)

The robot is always turning around one point, the instantaneous centre of rotation (ICC). It lies on the line through both wheel axles, on the side of the slower wheel. When vR = vL the ICC is infinitely far away (a straight line). When vR = −vL the ICC is exactly at the robot centre (a spin).

Example with the assumed 140 mm wheel base: vR = 1.0 m/s, vL = 0.6 m/s.

  • v = (1.0 + 0.6) / 2 = 0.8 m/s
  • ω = (1.0 − 0.6) / 0.14 = 2.86 rad/s, which is about 164° per second
  • R = 0.8 / 2.86 = 0.28 m, a gentle curve of 28 cm radius

Spinning with vR = +0.5 and vL = −0.5 m/s gives v = 0 and ω = 1.0 / 0.14 = 7.14 rad/s (about 409° per second).

Why the wheel base matters

A wider wheel base turns more slowly for the same speed difference (ω is divided by L) and is more stable, while a narrow one turns sharply and fits into tight mazes. The same L shows up in the odometry formulas of the encoder lessons (WHEEL_BASE_MM). Measure it between the middle of the two tyre contact patches, not between the outer edges of the wheels.

Remember that a line follower produces a steering command (from the PID in Lesson 06) and then converts it into vL = base − correction and vR = base + correction. That is differential drive in one line.

Wheel selection

The wheel is the last part of the drive chain and the only part that touches the track, so it deserves as much thought as the motor. You choose five things: diameter, width, material, tread and how it fixes to the shaft.

Diameter

Bigger wheels give a higher top speed at the same RPM (v = RPM × π × D / 60) but need more torque from the motor, and the robot sits higher. Smaller wheels give more force and finer control but less speed.

Width

A wider tyre gives a larger contact patch and often more grip on a smooth floor. Too wide and the robot scrubs sideways when it turns, which wastes energy and makes spins inaccurate.

Material

Soft rubber or silicone gives the best grip on clean tracks. Hard plastic wheels slip easily. A soft tyre can also wear and pick up dust, so keep it clean.

Tread

Smooth or lightly grooved tyres are normally best on a flat indoor mat. Deep treads are for rough ground and can reduce the contact area on a smooth surface.

Wheels also change your encoder resolution

An encoder counts shaft rotations, not millimetres. The distance covered per count depends on the wheel diameter, so a bigger wheel gives coarser distance measurement. Using the number of counts per wheel revolution from the encoder lessons (1400) as an example:

A simple choice for a competition robot

Start with a rubber or silicone-tyred wheel that is as small as your speed target allows, with a reasonably wide tyre and almost no tread. Check that the wheel is perfectly round and sits on the shaft without wobble. A wobbly wheel makes the robot waver even when the code is perfect.

RPM and speed

RPM means revolutions per minute: how fast the wheel shaft turns. To know how fast the robot moves you need the wheel size. In one revolution the wheel rolls forward by its circumference, π × D. So:

v (m/s) = RPM × π × D (m) / 60

Equivalently, with angular speed ω = 2π × RPM / 60 in rad/s, the speed is v = ω × r.

The 500 RPM, 65 mm example

  • Circumference: π × 0.065 = 0.2042 m
  • Revolutions per second: 500 / 60 = 8.33
  • Speed: 0.2042 × 8.33 = 1.70 m/s

Going the other way, to drive at 1.0 m/s with this wheel you need 60 / 0.2042 = 294 RPM at the wheel.

No-load RPM is the best case

The 500 RPM on the label is the speed with no load and at the rated voltage. Under load (the weight of the robot, friction, the wheel on the floor) the motor runs slower, and with a battery that has dropped below 12 V it runs slower again. In the examples below we assume about 80 % of the label speed in real use, so 1.70 m/s becomes roughly 1.36 m/s. Measure it on your robot: drive a marked 2 m distance and time it.

A DC motor's speed is roughly proportional to the voltage across it. That is why PWM duty cycle works as a speed control, and why speed changes as the battery drains. Closed-loop speed control with encoders (a later lesson) removes this drift.

Torque and force

Torque is the twisting effort of a motor shaft. A wheel turns that twist into a push on the ground. At the edge of a wheel with radius r:

F = T / r        (force at the ground, in newtons, torque in N·m, radius in m)

A small wheel turns the same torque into a bigger force, which is why small wheels accelerate and climb better, while large wheels go faster.

Units: N·m and kg·cm

Hobby motor datasheets quote torque in kg·cm: the force of a mass in kilograms at a lever arm of 1 cm. The SI unit is the newton-metre.

1 kg·cm = 0.0981 N·m        1 N·m = 10.2 kg·cm

How much force does the robot need?

Newton's second law gives the force to accelerate a mass m: F = m × a. To climb a slope of angle θ you also need F = m × g × sin(θ) (with g = 9.81 m/s²). Add rolling friction and a safety margin on top; start with 1.5 to 2 times the number you calculate.

Using the assumed robot (1.2 kg, 65 mm wheels, two motors each giving 1.0 kg·cm usable):

  • Total torque: 2 × 1.0 kg·cm = 2 × 0.0981 = 0.196 N·m
  • Push at the ground: F = 0.196 / 0.0325 = 6.03 N
  • Acceleration the motors could give: a = 6.03 / 1.2 = 5.0 m/s² (but see the traction limit below)
Stall torque is not working torque

The stall torque on a datasheet is what the motor makes when the shaft is held still, and it comes with the maximum (stall) current. You cannot run there: the motor and the driver would overheat. Choose the motor so that your normal needs sit at a modest fraction of the continuous rating, and use the stall figure only to check that your driver and battery survive the worst case (Lesson 11 covers the current side).

Gear ratio

A DC motor by itself spins fast with little torque. A gearbox trades speed for torque. The gear ratio is the number of teeth on the output gear divided by the teeth on the input gear. With a ratio of n : 1:

speed out  = speed in / n
torque out = torque in × n × efficiency

Efficiency is below 1 because gears lose energy to friction. A single spur-gear stage may be around 90 % and a long cheap gearbox much less; the exact number is not given on most hobby datasheets, so treat it as an estimate.

The trade-off

Higher ratioLower ratio
Slower robotFaster robot
More pushing force, easier slopesWeaker acceleration, may stall on slopes

Example: external gears

Say you want to drive at about 1.0 m/s but the motor/wheel combination gives 1.70 m/s with too little acceleration. A 2 : 1 external reduction gives:

  • Wheel speed: 500 / 2 = 250 RPM, so v = 250 × π × 0.065 / 60 = 0.85 m/s (no load)
  • Torque at the wheel: 1.0 × 2 × 0.9 = 1.8 kg·cm per motor (assuming 90 % efficiency)

Speed fell to half, force nearly doubled. For a different target, choose the ratio from n = RPMmotor / RPMwheel needed.

Backlash and where to put the gears

  • Backlash is the small free play between meshing gear teeth. It is why a geared wheel can wiggle a little before the motor shaft moves. It hurts precise turns and encoder-based position control; in a drive wheel with a continuous controller it is usually tolerable.
  • Motor gearbox (built-in): Johnson-type motors already include one. It is compact, sealed and simple, but the ratio is fixed.
  • External gears, chain or belt: let you fine-tune speed and torque, and move the motor away from the wheel. They add parts, alignment work, noise and more backlash. Use them only if the built-in ratio really does not fit.
Encoder placement

If your encoder sits on the motor shaft (before the gearbox), it gives many counts per wheel revolution (its counts multiply by the gear ratio), but it cannot see backlash in the gearbox. An encoder on the wheel side measures the wheel itself. Know which one you have before you set TICKS_PER_REV.

Friction and traction

Motor force only moves the robot if the tyres can push on the ground. Traction is the grip between tyre and floor, and it comes from friction.

  • Normal force N: how hard the floor pushes up on the wheel, equal to the weight resting on it. Only the weight on the driven wheels counts for driving.
  • Friction coefficient μ: a number that describes the pair of surfaces. Clean soft rubber on a smooth dry surface might be around 0.5 to 1; dust or oil can drop it a lot. Never trust a table, measure it.
  • Maximum traction force: Fmax = μ × N.
  • Static vs kinetic: while the tyre does not slip, the grip is static friction, which is the larger one. Once it slides, kinetic friction takes over and it is somewhat lower (the orange step in the diagram). So after a wheel starts to slip it is hard to recover.

Measure μ with a ramp

Put the robot (or a spare tyre pressed on a block) on a board of the same material as the track. Slowly raise one end. The angle θ at which it just starts to slide gives μ = tan(θ). For example, sliding at 35° gives μ = tan 35° = 0.70. It is rough, but it uses your real tyres and your real track.

Traction limits acceleration

For the assumed robot: N = 0.6 × 1.2 × 9.81 = 7.06 N on the driven wheels, so Fmax = 0.7 × 7.06 = 4.94 N and the best acceleration is amax = 4.94 / 1.2 = 4.12 m/s². The motors could give 5.0 m/s², so traction is the limit: pushing harder would only spin the wheels. The same limit applies to braking with the motors.

Slip ruins odometry

Encoders count wheel rotation, not distance travelled. When a wheel slips, the encoder reports movement that did not happen and your position estimate drifts. Keep acceleration inside the traction limit (use a ramp in your speed command, not a sudden jump to full PWM), and in turns avoid more speed than the tyres can hold.

Keep the tyres clean

  • Dust on the track and tyres is the usual reason a robot suddenly loses grip at a competition.
  • Wipe the tyres with a clean damp cloth or press a strip of sticky tape on them and peel it off. Let them dry before running.
  • Test any cleaning liquid on a spare tyre first; some chemicals can dry out or damage rubber.

Centre of gravity

The centre of gravity (CoG) is the point where the whole weight of the robot can be considered to act. Where it sits decides how the weight is shared between the wheels and whether the robot can tip.

The third contact point

With two driven wheels the robot needs a third point on the ground. A ball caster rolls in any direction and adds little friction, but it can catch on gaps; a skid (a smooth plastic or Teflon-like pad, or a smooth screw head) is simple and light but adds drag. Keep it at the same height as the contact patches so that the body stays level.

Where to put the heavy parts

  • Battery and motors low: low weight means a low CoG (small h), which is harder to tip.
  • Over the driven wheels: weight on the drive wheels increases the normal force and therefore traction. With the split b / (a + b) in the diagram, a = 60 mm and b = 90 mm put 90 / 150 = 60 % of the weight on the driven wheels.
  • Not too far back: if the CoG is behind the driven axle the robot becomes unstable and tends to sit on the skid.
  • Keep it centred left to right, so that both wheels carry the same load. Otherwise one wheel slips first and the robot pulls to one side.

Tipping, accelerating and braking

A robot tips when its acceleration is large compared with how low and how central its CoG is. With the CoG a distance a ahead of the driven axle and at height h, it starts to tip backwards when accelerating at more than atip = g × a / h. For a = 60 mm, h = 65 mm: atip = 9.81 × 60 / 65 = 9.1 m/s², well above the 4.1 m/s² traction limit, so the wheels slip long before it tips. If the heavy parts were mounted twice as high (h = 130 mm) the tipping limit would drop to 4.5 m/s², close to the traction limit.

Weight also moves when speed changes: braking pushes weight forward onto the caster and takes load away from the driven wheels, so they have less grip exactly when you need it. A low CoG reduces this weight transfer. This is one more reason to brake gradually before a tight corner.

Find your CoG

Balance the robot (with battery) on a ruler edge or a pencil in each direction. The balance point along the length gives your a and b. Move the battery by a few millimetres and see how it changes.

Design checklist for a line follower or maze robot

  • Decide the target speed first. Compute the wheel RPM needed (RPM = 60 × v / (π × D)) and pick the motor ratio for that.
  • Check the force: does the motor deliver at least 1.5 to 2 times the torque for your acceleration and slope, without running near stall?
  • Keep the acceleration below the traction limit μ × N / m. Add speed ramps in code.
  • Use soft rubber or silicone tyres on the driven wheels, clean them before every run and measure μ on the real track.
  • Put the battery and motors low and put 55 to 65 % of the weight on the driven wheels (adjust to your own tests). Keep left and right balanced.
  • Wheel base: wide enough for stability, narrow enough for tight corners and the sensor array.
  • Check wheels are round, mounted without wobble, and that the caster or skid does not drag or catch.
  • Update WHEEL_DIAMETER_MM, WHEEL_BASE_MM and TICKS_PER_REV in your code to match the real robot.
  • Test speed with a stopwatch on a measured distance and compare to your calculation.

Key takeaways

  • Differential drive steers with two wheel speeds: v = (vR + vL) / 2, ω = (vR − vL) / L. Equal speeds go straight, unequal speeds curve, opposite speeds spin.
  • Wheels: diameter trades speed against force, soft rubber gives grip, and the diameter also changes distance per encoder count.
  • Speed is v = RPM × π × D / 60. Label RPM is no-load; real speed is lower.
  • Force at the ground is F = T / r. 1 kg·cm is about 0.098 N·m. Do not design near the stall torque.
  • Gear ratio divides speed and multiplies torque (less friction losses). Backlash is the cost.
  • Traction is limited to μ × N. Past that the wheels slip and odometry becomes wrong.
  • Centre of gravity: keep it low and over the driven wheels, and watch weight transfer when braking.
  • Every motor figure in this lesson was an example assumption. Use your own datasheet and measurements.
